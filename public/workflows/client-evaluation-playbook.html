The PR Hub · How we run a client evaluation

The client evaluation check-in

The senior half-hour that protects the relationship — held every three to six months, above the day-to-day team.

Who this is for: the founder or a senior principal running the check-in — deliberately someone outside the day-to-day account team, so the client can be candid. It takes you from spotting a client is due, through the two emails that set it up, to running the conversation, scoring it, and closing the loop with the account team.

The one idea to hold onto

This meeting exists to hear the client — not to defend the work.

A client will tell a senior voice from outside the weekly grind things they'd never raise in a WIP. That candour is the whole point. We're not here to justify what we've done or sell the next thing — we're here to ask how it's really landing, listen without flinching, and make them feel heard and valued. Not everything goes 100%, and an agency confident in its work isn't afraid to ask for the feedback and act on it. Run well, this is the meeting that turns a good account into a long one.

How often — and why the range

Every three to six months. Never past six.

We tell clients we run these every three to six months. The range is deliberate: it lets you pick the right moment — after a campaign lands, when the team or scope has shifted, when the account needs a reset — rather than forcing a date on the calendar. The one hard rule: no account goes more than six months without one. If you're unsure whether it's time, it's time.

Part One
Set it up

From “they're due” to a date in the diary

Before the meeting

Setting up an evaluation is four moves: spot who's due, ask for a time, give them a gentle heads-up, and prep properly with the account team. Two emails do the client-facing work — the first books it, the second frames it — and neither asks the client to do any homework.

01

Step one · spot who's due

Check when they last had one

Open the Client Evaluation Meeting Feedback sheet and check the client's tab for the date of their last check-in. Anyone past the three-month mark is a candidate; anyone near six months is overdue.

Make it a habit, not a scramble

Drop a reminder in your calendar for roughly when each client's next check-in should fall, so these never sneak up on you.

Sense-check the timing with the account team before you reach out — they'll know if something on the account (a campaign mid-flight, a team change, a wobble) makes now exactly the right, or exactly the wrong, moment.

02

Step two · the booking email

Ask for a time

Keep it warm and low-friction. You're asking for half an hour of open conversation, not scheduling a performance review. Offer a couple of concrete times so it's easy to say yes.

Email 1 · book the check-inThe PR Hub
Subject: Time for a catch up

Hi [name],

I'd love to get 30 minutes in the diary to hear how things are going with us from your perspective. We do this from time to time with clients to understand what's working well, where you're seeing the most value, and anything we could be doing better.

It's also a good chance to hear what's coming up for [company] and make sure we're putting our time and energy into the things that matter most to you.

Would any of these times work? Nothing to prepare — just a chance for an open conversation. If not, let me know some alternatives and we'll find a slot.

[Day, date — time, time]
[Day, date — time, time]

Speak soon,
[your name]

Why it reads this way

"From time to time," not "quarterly review." Clients relax into a catch-up; they brace for an appraisal. The language sets the tone before the meeting starts.

Offer times, don't ask for availability. Two options with specific slots gets a reply far faster than an open "when suits?".

It's by Zoom, ~30 minutes. Say so if it helps — it signals this is light, not a big commitment.

03

Step three · lock it + the heads-up

Confirm, then set the frame

Once they've picked a time, send a calendar invite with the Zoom link and bcc the account team so they know it's happening. Then — and this is the piece that makes the conversation richer — send a short heads-up that gives them a feel for what you'll talk about. Not homework. Not a form. Just enough that they arrive knowing this is about making the partnership better.

Email 2 · the heads-up (send once booked)The PR Hub
Subject: Looking forward to our catch up

Hi [name],

Lovely — [day/time] it is, invite on its way.

Nothing at all to prepare, but so you know where I'm coming from: I just want to hear how you feel it's all going. We'll touch on how well you think we understand your business and where it's heading, how you rate the results and the quality of the work, how we're communicating, and — most usefully — anything you'd want us doing differently or more of.

We do this because we genuinely want to keep getting better at what we do for you. Not everything in PR goes exactly to plan, and we'd far rather hear the honest version so we can shape how we work together from here. It's a chance for you to be heard, and for us to make sure our energy is going where it matters most to [company].

Looking forward to it.

Warm regards,
[your name]

The line this email has to walk

Signal the themes, don't issue a questionnaire. "We'll touch on…" primes them to think; a numbered list of questions turns a catch-up into an exam.

Name the spirit out loud. That we want the honest version, that not everything goes to plan, that we're committed to improving — this is what makes a client open up rather than be polite.

04

Step four · prep with the team

Walk in across the account

The day before, speak with the account team to get the real picture — what's working, what's been bumpy, and where the gaps are on either side. Then read the current PR plan and the recent coverage so you can hold your own on the detail. You're running this independently of the team, but you're never walking in blind.

Get from the account team

  • What's genuinely working — so you can acknowledge it specifically, not generically.
  • Any live friction — a slow patch, a pitch that's stalled, a handover that felt bumpy. Know it before the client raises it.
  • Gaps on either side — where we're waiting on the client, and where we've dropped a ball. Honesty here makes you credible in the room.
  • What they'd love steered — the thing the team can't easily say to the client, but you can.
Part Two
Run the conversation

The half-hour itself

In the meeting

One-on-one with the client (anyone on their side is welcome), by Zoom. You're scoring four areas out of ten — but the scores are the scaffolding, not the point. The gold is in the "why" behind each number and in the deeper questions you ask around them. Open by getting a yes to record, so you can capture it faithfully.

Say this at the top — every time

"Thanks so much for the time. This is really just a chance for me to hear how it's all going from your side, so we can keep getting better for you. Nothing's off limits. Mind if I record it so I capture your feedback properly?" Wait for the yes. If they decline, take careful notes instead.

The four areas you score

Capture each in the Client Evaluation Meeting Feedback sheet — a score and the notes behind it. The number is a conversation-opener; the notes are the asset.

1 · Understanding your business objectives

/10

How well do we understand how your business works and where it's heading — and are we aligning the PR strategy to it?

2 · Results & outcomes vs goals

/10

Against the goals set with the account team, how do you rate the results and outcomes so far?

3 · Quality of work

/10

How do you rate the quality of the work the account team produces — the writing, the thinking, the delivery?

4 · Would you recommend us?

Y / N

Would you recommend The PR Hub to a friend or colleague? The single truest read on how the relationship really sits.

Then an open "anything else?" — often where the most useful feedback of the whole meeting surfaces.

The one scoring rule that matters

Score the period you're in — never show past scores. The number should reflect how we're doing now, not be anchored to what they gave last time. Don't read previous scores back to the client, and don't let them mark "an improvement on last time" — ask them to rate today, fresh.

The ten questions to dig deeper on

A score on its own tells you little. These ten prompts — drawn from what clients have actually raised in past evaluations — are where you probe. You won't use all ten every time; pick the ones that fit the account and follow the thread wherever it goes. Ask open, then stay quiet and let them talk.

  1. Has what you're trying to achieve shifted since we started — and are we building around where you're heading now? Objectives move: a pivot, a new market, a raise. The most common root cause of a soft "understanding" score is that we're still serving the old brief.
  2. Are we bringing you enough ideas, or do you feel you're having to chase us? Clients consistently want proactivity — to be led, not just be responsive. "Chase us more, bring more forward" is a recurring note. Find out where they want us out in front.
  3. When a piece of coverage lands, is it clear to you what it actually did for the business? Activity without a line back to business impact erodes perceived value. Probe how they judge whether PR is working — and whether we're drawing that line for them.
  4. Are we agreed on what success looks like this period — and are we tracking to it? Fuzzy or unshared KPIs are behind a lot of middling result scores. If success was never clearly defined, that's the conversation to have — ideally with the team afterwards.
  5. Do you feel we've got enough live in the market, or are we circling the same angle too long? Momentum matters to how clients feel about value. "We seem to be circling one pitch" and "I'd like to see more active pitches" come up — test the sense of pace.
  6. Is our coverage reaching the audiences that matter to you — or the same ones each time? Clients worry about echo chambers and about landing in the right outlets for their sector. Ask where they want to be seen that we're not getting them yet.
  7. Does the writing sound like you, and land where it needs to? Quality scores swing most on written work — voice, thought leadership, getting the language right. Find out where it's nailed it and where it's missed.
  8. Are you hearing what's being said in your market from us first — or from elsewhere? Clients expect their PR agency to be their eyes and ears. Hearing about coverage or market chatter from the market first is a quiet but real dissatisfier.
  9. Is the way and the rhythm we communicate working for you — channels, meetings, reports? Preferences differ wildly: some want email only, some find Slack too much, some want fewer meetings and just the notes. Tailoring this is an easy, high-trust win.
  10. Through any team or account changes, has it stayed clear who's doing what — and that you're well looked after? Handovers and transitions are where confidence leaks. If a change felt bumpy, name it, own it, and reassure them on continuity.
Part Three
Close the loop

Turning candour into change

After the meeting

Feedback the account never sees is feedback wasted. The meeting isn't done when the call ends — it's done when the team knows what was said and what changes because of it. Four moves: tidy the notes, debrief with the team, write it up, and file it.

01

Straight after

Tidy the notes

While it's fresh, clean up the scores and comments in the Client Evaluation Meeting Feedback sheet. Make sure every score has the "why" against it and the open comments are captured faithfully — in the client's words where they said something that matters.

02

Within a few days

Debrief with the team

Set a short meeting with the account lead and leadership to walk through what came up — the wins to keep doing, the friction to fix, and anything that needs a genuine change in how we run the account. This is where feedback becomes action, not a filed form.

Feedback wasted

Scores logged in the sheet, nobody told, nothing changes. Next check-in, the client raises the same thing — and now they're frustrated they said it once already.

Feedback that lands

The team hears it within days, owns the fixable things, and the client sees the change before the next catch-up. That's what keeps clients for years.

03

After the debrief

Write it up & file it

Write up the follow-up notes from the debrief — the feedback, the decisions, and what each person is picking up — so the account team has a reference to act on and to raise in future client meetings. Then file it per the Client Drive Folder Map:

SHARED → REPORTING → EVALUATIONS

Name it date-led so it sorts itself: 2026-11-01_[Client]_Evaluation_Notes. The running scores stay in the Client Evaluation Meeting Feedback sheet; the written follow-up lives in the client's Drive folder.

Before, during, after

The evaluation checklist

Run this each time. Tick all six and you ran it like the senior voice the client needs to hear from.

  • I checked when they were last evaluated, sense-checked the timing with the account team, and no client is past six months.
  • I sent the warm booking email with two concrete times, then the heads-up email that framed the spirit without setting homework.
  • I prepped with the account team — what's working, the friction, the gaps — and read the current plan and recent coverage.
  • I got a yes to record, scored all four areas for the current period only, and never read back past scores.
  • I used the deeper questions to get past the number, listened more than I talked, and left them feeling genuinely heard.
  • I tidied the notes, debriefed the team within days, wrote up the follow-up, and filed it under REPORTING → EVALUATIONS.

Red flags — the tells of an evaluation run badly

  • It got pushed so long the client was overdue — or it was skipped because the account "seemed fine".
  • The email read like a performance review, or asked the client to fill something in beforehand.
  • You walked in without the team's read, so the client surfaced friction you should have known about.
  • You anchored the scores to last time, or defended the work instead of listening to the feedback.
  • You talked more than the client did — and left without the honest version.
  • The feedback sat in the sheet and never reached the team, so nothing actually changed.
