The PR Hub · How we win tier-one coverage

From brief to front page

The senior thinking behind a tier-one pitch — written down, so you can run it yourself.

Who this is for: anyone on the team handed a client's "big news" who needs to work out whether it's actually a story — and how to make an editor at a top-tier national masthead care.

Note — the worked example running through this guide is real in its mechanics but disguised in its details. Names, figures and specifics are illustrative; follow the thinking, not the particulars.

The one idea to hold onto

Announcements aren't news. Stories are.

A client hands you a partnership, a product, a launch — convinced it's front-page material because it's the biggest thing in their world. Your job isn't to relay their announcement. It's to find the story their news is evidence of — the trend, the shift, the David-and-Goliath fight, the number that makes an editor sit up. The client is too close to see it. That gap is exactly what they pay us for, and everything below is a repeatable way to close it.

01

Before you write a single word

Gather the whole landscape — client and media

The instinct under deadline is to open a blank doc and start drafting. Don't. You can't position a story until you know two things: exactly what the client is sitting on, and what the media around them already cares about this month. Read both before you form a single opinion.

Two piles to build

  1. The client pile — every doc they've sent: the approved announcement, the fact sheet, the stats. Read all of it, even the boring attachments.
  2. The live media pile — the last few months of coverage of the client and the other named party in a partnership. Real articles, with bylines and dates.

Read laterally — the best asset is rarely in the brief

The most valuable thing you find is often something the client didn't think to give you. Searching the media around the partner, the standout wasn't a story about either brand's partnership at all — it was a property-trade piece revealing the client had just spent roughly $10m on a major new site. That single fact became the backbone of the whole pitch. The client saw a partnership; the press already had proof of a growth story.

Worked example · a storage brand × a national retailer

The find

A property-trade article reported the client had quietly bought a large new site for ~$10m — the retail partner named only as a neighbour. Nobody briefed us on it. It reframed everything: not a company doing a deal, but a company on an eight-figure expansion tear. That's a business story.

02

Read the room like an editor would

Cluster the coverage into angles — then name the journalists

A pile of articles is noise until you sort it. Group recent coverage into distinct angles — the recurring lenses editors are using right now. For any big brand you'll usually find four to six. Then, for each angle, write down the named journalist and masthead. Pitches don't go to publications; they go to people.

For the retail partner, the coverage clustered cleanly into five recurring lenses:

  • Value / cost-of-living — winning while shoppers trade downTop-tier business masthead, retail desk (find the specific reporter who owns the round)
  • Retail innovation / quick-commerce — on-demand delivery, tens of thousands of SKUsA respected SME/business title — its named retail reporter
  • The trade / SME economy — a revamped loyalty program for business customersThe same SME/business title — same reporter
  • Community / social licence — grants, giving backA metro news desk
  • Cultural icon — the brand's cult community ritualsA mainstream news / culture site

Now find the through-line

Two questions decide your target: which angle has the most editorial oxygen right now, and which already has a warm, repeat byline you can approach? Here, "retail innovation" and "value" had both — and a named reporter who covers this beat repeatedly is your highest-probability yes while you chase the tier-one exclusive.

Junior move

"I'll send it to the top two national papers and a few retail sites." A generic blast to mastheads.

Senior move

"This maps to the innovation angle one named reporter owns, and the growth angle the national business press runs front-of-book — two people, two framings." Targeted by human and lens.

03

The core act of the job

Kill the announcement. Climb to the story.

Every piece of client news can be pitched at different altitudes. The lowest is the announcement — the weakest, most ignorable frame, and the one the client will hand you. Your job is to climb until you reach a frame an editor can't ignore.

"Storage brand partners with national retailer"An announcement. Two brands did a thing.
Weak · page-filler
↓
"Retailers race to own convenience"A trend. Your news becomes an example inside it.
Better · trade interest
↓
"Homegrown challenger takes on the storage giants — from a big-box car park"A growth-and-challenger story. Money, scale, a Goliath.
Front-of-book

Why the top rung wins, transparently: business editors don't care about a service integration. They care about a scrappy local company using the country's most-trusted retail brand as a weapon against entrenched incumbents (the big self-storage chains), backed by proof it's spending eight figures to grow. The partnership stops being the story and becomes the strategy inside a bigger one.

The reframe test

Before you pitch anything, say the headline out loud and ask: "Would an editor run this if it were about a company they'd never heard of?" If the only interesting thing is the brand names, you're still on the bottom rung. Climb.

04

What separates a pitch from an exclusive

Run the tier-one gap analysis

The top-tier national business press won't run a story on vibes. An exclusive needs a specific kit. List what the story needs, mark what you already have, and the gaps become your exact ask to the client. Five things unlock a front-of-book exclusive:

  • A scale number — how big, how fast (store count, national footprint, timeframe).Converts a partnership into a growth story.
  • One defensible superlative — first / biggest / only.Editors need a single claim they can stand behind in a headline.
  • A Goliath — who is the client challenging?No incumbent to disrupt, no tension, no story.
  • Named senior access — an exec on the record; ideally both sides.A partner-brand exec quote is worth more than any stat, and is what makes "exclusive" real.
  • The human + strategic "why" — the founder, the customer problem, the fit.The narrative that makes it a feature, not a notice.

Junior move

Asks the client for "any stats you can share" and builds the pitch around whatever comes back.

Senior move

Knows the five things an exclusive requires, audits what's missing, and asks the client only for those — precisely, in priority order.

Work within the client's limits. Say the client won't share revenue figures — fine. Growth rate year-on-year, the ~$10m site, units deployed and jobs created all carry a growth story without a single revenue dollar. Never let a "no" on one number stall the pitch; find the proof point they will give you.

05

When the news is already half-out

Neutralise the leak — de-news the old version

Sometimes the story has already surfaced — a niche trade or tech site ran a version of it. Panic says the exclusive is dead. It isn't. A small write-up only burns a tier-one exclusive if your frame and data are the same as theirs. Change both and it reads as a bigger, different story.

  1. Change the frame. The trade site covered the "what" (an integration went live). You pitch the "how big / how fast / what it means" — the growth story. Different lane, not a rehash.
  2. Bring assets they never had. Growth rate, the scale number, the ~$10m expansion, exec access. New material = new story in an editor's eyes.
  3. Bundle the old news as one proof point inside the bigger narrative, so even a reader who saw the leak meets a fundamentally larger story.

Diagnose before you write

You can't neutralise a leak you haven't read. Get the exact article; note the precise words and framing it used — the re-frame depends on knowing what "old" now looks like. Only then decide how to make yours read as new.

06

How to ask without burning the client

Extract with double-duty questions

When you go back to the client for the missing pieces, every question should do two jobs at once: pull an asset the pitch needs, and signal we're already thinking three moves ahead. A good question is also a status update — it shows sophistication, and buys trust and speed.

The formula

Open with a line that frames the ask as an update, not an interrogation. Then keep it to a maximum of five — a short, sharp list gets answered; a long one gets "let me come back to you."

Opening line · reframes the ask as progress
"We've mapped the current [partner] media landscape and see a clear lane to take this beyond a partnership announcement into a national growth-and-challenger story — the kind the top-tier national business press runs front-of-book. To build that pitch, there are five things we'd love your steer on."

Then the five, ranked by how much each unlocks. Notice how the fourth name-drops a fact we found — instant proof we've done the homework:

  1. How big does the rollout get, and how fast? Store count, timeframe, national footprint.The single number that makes it a growth story.
  2. How far will the partner go publicly — a named exec quote, or a joint announcement?Framed for a soft yes, not a hard co-approval. Enough to anchor an exclusive.
  3. Is this a first-of-its-kind — and who are we effectively challenging?Gets the superlative and the Goliath in one.
  4. Beyond YoY growth, what momentum can we put on the record? The recent site acquisition, units, jobs.Signals we already track their growth in the trade press.
  5. Who is this really for, and is the founder available to tell that story?The human "why," and the access an exclusive needs.

Junior move

Sends a 12-point questionnaire covering everything, and waits a week for a partial reply.

Senior move

Five questions, ranked, each also a signal of strategy — and flags which three unlock a draft, so the client knows what to prioritise.

07

Write the hook before the pitch

Lead with the headline you want them to write

Don't write the pitch and hope a headline falls out. Write the headline you want the journalist to run first — it forces you to prove you have a story, and becomes the spine everything hangs on. Be creative, be transparent about why media will care, and give them a line they could almost publish as-is.

Worked example · the headline territory

"The homegrown storage disruptor taking on the self-storage giants — from a big-box car park."

Why it works: a named Goliath (the big self-storage chains), a David (the homegrown challenger), a vivid ownable image (the retailer's car park), and momentum behind it (the ~$10m expansion). The partnership is in the headline but isn't the point — the growth fight is. That's what gets an editor to reply.

Hold headlines as territories, not final copy, until the client confirms the facts. Lock the wording to real, checked numbers — we don't need approvals to think, but the data has to be right before it goes out.

Before it leaves the building

The pre-pitch checklist

  • I've read all the client material and the last few months of live coverage — including the partner's.
  • I can name the angle and the specific journalist I'm pitching, and why them.
  • My headline survives the "unknown company" test — it's not just famous brand names.
  • I have a scale number, a superlative, a Goliath, named access, and a human "why" — or a clear plan to get each.
  • If the news has leaked, my frame and my assets are demonstrably different from what's already out.
  • Every fact and figure is checked. We think freely; we publish accurately.

Red flags — the tells of a junior pitch

  • The headline only works because the brands are famous.
  • You're pitching a publication, not a person.
  • The whole thing rests on one stat the client hasn't confirmed.
  • You started writing before you'd read the media landscape.
  • You accepted the client's framing without climbing the ladder.
