The PR Hub · How we run a first conversation

The 30-minute discovery call

One call, run to a script — so a prospect leaves feeling understood, and we leave knowing whether it's a fit.

Who this is for: anyone on the team who books and runs a first conversation with a potential new client. It gives you the email to set it up, the questions to ask (and what to listen for), and the framework we send the prospect beforehand.

The one idea to hold onto

A discovery call isn't a pitch. It's a diagnosis.

In thirty minutes we are not trying to sell — we're trying to understand a business well enough to know if we can move its needle, and to make the person in front of us feel genuinely heard. We send six areas in advance so they arrive relaxed, not revising. The framework is the floor, not the ceiling: it guarantees we cover the ground, and it frees you to follow the thread that matters. If you spend the call reading questions off a list, you've missed the point.

The areas we'd love to explore:

Tailoring rules

Keep it under one screen. If it scrolls, they'll skim. Six areas, one reassurance, one sign-off.

Warm the top, never the list. Personalise the opening line to them; leave the six areas broadly as-is — they're deliberately universal, and rewriting them per prospect is where this goes wrong.

Send it 2–3 days out. Far enough that it's not homework the night before; close enough that the call is real in their mind.

Part Two
The question framework

How you actually run the 30 minutes

Internal — never sent

This is your side of the call, and it stays internal. For each area you get: the ask (how to open it out loud), listen for (the signals that tell you what kind of business — and person — you're dealing with), and tweak if (how to bend the questions when a signal fires). Times are a guide to keep you honest against the clock, not a stopwatch.

The clock — a 30-minute shape

0–2 min · Open & frame — warm welcome, restate the shape ("I'll run us through those six areas, then we'll talk next steps"), and get a yes. · 2–26 min · The six areas, ~4 min each. · 26–30 min · Close & next steps.

If you're running long, the two areas you can compress are 1 (business today) and 5 (founder story) — you can pull those from research and a follow-up. Never sacrifice 2, 3 or 6: heading, audience and the PR opportunity are the three that tell you if there's a mandate here.

01

Area one · orient yourself fast

The business today

~4 min
The ask — open it like thisBefore we get into where you're going, give me the shape of the business as it is right now — how you'd describe it to someone who's never heard of you, and what's genuinely changed in the last year.
  1. In a sentence, what does the business do — and for whom?Their one-liner tells you how clearly they see themselves. A rambling answer is itself a finding.
  2. How big are you now — team, customers, revenue band, markets?You're sizing the opportunity and the budget reality without asking about budget.
  3. What's materially changed in the last 12 months — grown, launched, shifted, hired?Change is where stories and momentum live. Flat means you'll have to manufacture news.

Listen for

  • Momentum vs. plateau. "We doubled" / "we just entered [market]" = fuel. "Steady, ticking along" = you'll need to work harder for angles.
  • How they talk about customers. Named, specific, proud — or vague? Tells you whether case studies exist.
  • Numbers they volunteer. The figures they reach for unprompted are the ones they're proud of — and the ones a journalist will care about too.
  • Category confidence. Do they own their space, or hedge ("sort of a...")? Positioning work may be the real job.

Tweak if…

If they undersell it → mirror it back bigger: "So you've gone from X to Y in a year — that's a growth story most of your competitors would kill for." Watch whether they light up or resist. That reaction is data.

If they oversell / it's all vision, no substance → gently anchor: "And where's that showing up in the numbers today?" You need to know if there's a there there before we attach our name to it.

02

Area two · find the commercial engine

Where you're heading

~4 min
The ask — open it like thisNow the next 12 to 18 months — what are the one or two things that, if they happen, make it a great year? And where would being better known actually help you get there?
  1. What are your top priorities over the next 12–18 months?Anchor everything else to this. PR that doesn't serve the priority is a vanity exercise.
  2. Where would greater visibility, credibility or authority make a commercial difference?This is the money question of the whole call. It converts "we want PR" into "we want X outcome."
  3. What's standing in the way of that today?The obstacle is often exactly what we're being hired to move — a trust gap, an awareness gap, a category we don't yet own.

Listen for

  • A commercial goal, not a PR goal. "Raise a round" / "enter [market]" / "win enterprise deals" is gold. "Get more coverage" is a symptom — dig past it to the outcome underneath.
  • Visibility, credibility, or authority — which one? They're different jobs. Awareness (nobody knows us), trust (they know us but don't believe us), or authority (be the name in the category). Name which they actually need.
  • A deadline or a trigger. A raise, a launch, a board mandate. Urgency changes tier and timeline — and closes faster.
  • Realism. Do the ambition and the resources line up? A moonshot on a shoestring is a red flag worth surfacing kindly.

Tweak if…

If they only give you activity ("we want to be in the press") → ladder up: "And if that lands perfectly — what does it unlock for the business?" Keep asking "so that…?" until you hit a commercial outcome.

If they name a hard trigger (raise / launch / sale) → pivot to the timeline immediately: "When does that need to land?" This reshapes the entire proposal and often the tier.

03

Area three · define the target

Who you need to reach

~4 min
The ask — open it like thisThink about the people you most need to find you and trust you — customers, partners, investors, talent. When one of them Googles you or asks an AI about your space, what do you need them to walk away believing?
  1. Who are the audiences that matter most — and which is the priority?Everyone says "everyone." Force a rank. The priority audience dictates which media and channels we go after.
  2. Where do they already look for information and who do they already trust?Tells you the outlets, formats and voices that actually reach them — not the ones that flatter the client.
  3. When they discover you through media, search or AI, what should they immediately understand?This is the message we'll be engineering. Their answer is the raw material for positioning.

Listen for

  • Specificity of audience. "CFOs at mid-market SaaS firms" is workable; "businesses" is not. Vagueness here means positioning work comes first.
  • The discovery gap. What they want understood vs. what actually shows up today when you search them. That gap is the brief.
  • AI / search awareness. Do they get that buyers now ask AI platforms about their category? Modern framing is a differentiator we can lead with.
  • B2B vs. B2C vs. investor/talent. Each points at a completely different media strategy. Note which dominates.

Tweak if…

If they list five audiences → "If we could only move the needle with one this year, which one?" The forced choice reveals the real commercial driver.

If they've never thought about search / AI discovery → plant the seed lightly: "Have you looked at what comes up when someone asks ChatGPT about [category]?" — it reframes PR as something bigger than press, and it's often the moment they lean in.

04

Area four · the perception gap

What you want to be known for

~4 min
The ask — open it like thisTwo versions of this — how do you think the market sees you today, honestly? And in three to five years, what do you want to be the name for?
  1. How is the business perceived today?Their read on their own reputation. Compare it to what you found in research — the gap is telling.
  2. What's misunderstood or underappreciated about you?Often the single richest vein in the whole call — the untold story they're frustrated nobody sees.
  3. In 3–5 years, what do you want to be known for?The destination for the positioning. Everything we do should bend the perception toward this.

Listen for

  • The frustration. When they get animated about being misread, you've found the emotional core of the engagement — and usually the best story.
  • Gap between current and aspirational. Small gap = amplification job. Big gap = repositioning job. Very different scopes and timelines.
  • A category they want to own. "The go-to for X" is authority-building — our highest-value, stickiest work.
  • Founder ego vs. business need. Is "known for" about the founder's profile or the company's commercial position? Both are valid — just name which, so the proposal aims right.

Tweak if…

If they can't articulate a 3–5 year "known for" → that IS the finding — say so warmly: "That's honestly one of the most valuable things we help clients define." You've just scoped positioning work.

If current perception is actively negative → slow down and probe gently — is there a reputation issue in play? That changes this from growth PR to something more sensitive, and it needs to surface now, not after signing.

05

Area five · the human material

The founder story

~4 min
The ask — open it like thisI always want to understand the why. What made you start this — what did you see, or what were you frustrated by? And how did the founding team come together?
  1. Why did you start it — what did you see that others didn't?Origin stories are the most portable, media-friendly asset a founder-led business has.
  2. How did the founders come together?The team's chemistry and complementarity is often a story in itself — and a credibility signal.
  3. What beliefs or experiences shape how you operate?This is the values layer — the thread for thought leadership and the founder's personal brand.

Listen for

  • A moment, not a mission statement. The specific incident — the lived frustration, the turned-down deal — is what makes press. Steer them from abstract to anecdote.
  • Founder as spokesperson potential. Are they warm, quotable, willing to be visible? A media-able founder massively expands what we can do.
  • Conviction / contrarian belief. A strong, spiky point of view is the seed of every thought-leadership program.
  • Sensitivities. Co-founder splits, past failures, personal hardship — handle with care, but note them; they're often the strongest and most delicate material.

Tweak if…

If they're modest / "it's not that interesting" → reassure and dig: "The best founder stories always sound ordinary from the inside — walk me through the actual moment you decided to do this." Almost always unlocks the gold.

If they light up and run long → let them. This is the area where over-time is a good sign — you can borrow the minutes back from Area 1. Just don't lose the close.

06

Area six · the proof and the prize

The opportunity for PR

~4 min
The ask — open it like thisLast one, and it's the practical one. What have you got that could bring this story to life — results, data, partnerships, milestones? And if we did brilliant work together, what would it ultimately have helped you achieve?
  1. What proof points do you have — results, data, partnerships, milestones, awards?Our raw ammunition. No proof = we build proof first, which is a different (and slower) engagement.
  2. Is there data or insight only you have?Proprietary data is the single most powerful earned-media asset a business can own. Always ask.
  3. What would a successful engagement ultimately achieve for the business?Their definition of success = how we'll be measured. Close the loop back to Area 2's commercial goal.

Listen for

  • Hard, checkable assets. Real numbers, named partners, dated milestones — the things a journalist can stand behind. Note every one.
  • Proprietary data. If they sit on usage data, market insight, a survey they could run — flag it hard. This alone can anchor a whole program.
  • How they define success. Coverage volume? Lead quality? A specific outcome? This is the KPI conversation starting — capture it exactly.
  • Expectation mismatch. If they expect the front page next week off zero assets, that gap needs a gentle, honest reset — better now than in month two.

Tweak if…

If they're asset-rich → get specific and greedy: "Could you share the last 12 months of numbers before we speak again?" You're already scoping the first campaign.

If they're asset-light → reframe, don't deflate: "Part of the early work is often building the proof points that don't exist yet — and that's very doable." Sets honest expectations and keeps the door open.

→

The last four minutes

Close, and set the next step

~3 min

Never let the call fade out. Land it deliberately: reflect, gauge, and name the next move.

  1. Reflect it back in one line. "So the headline for me is — you're [growing fast / at an inflection], you want to be known for [X] with [audience], and the real prize is [commercial goal]." Watch them nod or correct. Either way you win.
  2. One open question. "Is there anything important I haven't asked about?" The best material sometimes arrives here.
  3. Name the next step and own it. "Here's what I'll do: I'll pull together [a short point of view / some initial thinking] and come back to you by [day]." Concrete, dated, ours to deliver.

Junior close

"Great, this was really helpful — we'll be in touch!" Vague, passive, forgettable. The lead cools.

Senior close

Reflects the business back sharper than they said it, then commits to a specific, dated next step they didn't have to ask for. They leave feeling understood and pulled forward.

Straight after — while it's warm

Spend ten minutes now, not tomorrow: write up the six areas against what you heard, flag the commercial goal, the priority audience, the best story, and the proof points. Note your read on fit and tier, and the one thing that would make this a yes or a no. Then do the next step you promised, on time. The follow-through is the pitch.

Before, during, after

The discovery-call checklist

Run this in your head each time. If you can tick all six, you ran it like a senior.

  • I sent the set-up email 2–3 days out, tailored at the top, six areas left intact.
  • I did 15 minutes of research first — I know their category, their news, and what shows up when you search them.
  • I let them talk. I followed the thread, not the script — the list was my floor, not my cage.
  • I left with the commercial goal, the priority audience, the best story, and the proof points.
  • I reflected the business back sharper than they said it, and named a specific, dated next step.
  • I wrote it up while it was warm, and I did the next step on time.

Red flags — the tells of a junior call

  • You read the questions off the list, in order, like a form.
  • You talked more than they did, or pitched The PR Hub before you understood the business.
  • You left with "we want more coverage" and never laddered up to the commercial goal.
  • You accepted "everyone" as the target audience.
  • You ended with "we'll be in touch" and no dated next step.
  • You promised a follow-up and let it slip.
